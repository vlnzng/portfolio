## 01 · Context

This project was my master's thesis at TH Brandenburg. It addressed a practical design-stage question: how can UX designers support fair monetisation in video games while balancing ethical standards, regulatory requirements and commercial viability?

The timing is relevant. Monetisation has become part of the player experience, while EU consumer-protection enforcement increasingly targets unclear pricing, frictionless spending, pressure cues and weak safeguards for minors. Teams need ways to spot risk-sensitive design properties early, before a flow becomes expensive to change.

## 02 · Problem

Monetisation decisions are made through interface design: how prices are shown, how offers are framed, how easy it is to accept or decline, and how much pressure a player feels at the point of decision.

Legal review usually happens late, while design teams work in different terms: clarity, friction, urgency, consent, player control. This creates a gap. Fairness risks can stay invisible until a flow is already built, even though many of them are visible directly in the interface.

## 03 · Process

I synthesised four perspectives that are usually discussed separately: monetisation economics, player spending psychology, UX research on dark patterns, and EU consumer-protection expectations.

The core challenge was translation. I turned abstract legal and ethical expectations into inspectable indicators, answerable by looking at the user journey itself, with no backend access, no analytics, and no assumptions about developer intent.

<img class="case-diagram" src="/assets/showcases/case1/synthesis.svg" alt="Four research perspectives (monetisation economics, player psychology, dark patterns and UX, EU consumer law) converging into one set of inspectable design indicators." width="1600" height="780" loading="lazy" />

Three examples:

- **Real-money price visibility:** Can players see a real-money price, or a clear equivalent, at the point of decision?
- **Friction symmetry:** Is it no harder to decline or cancel than to accept or start?
- **Protection of minors:** Does the design avoid directly urging minors to buy, or pressuring adults into it?

## 04 · Solution

A live, web-based assessment tool. A team runs one monetisation flow through three steps: context, 12 guided questions, result.

<img class="case-shot" src="/assets/showcases/case1/questions.webp" alt="Guided questions in the tool, each answered Yes, No or Unsure, for the price and currency clarity domain." width="1600" height="684" loading="lazy" />

The tool returns an overall concern level, from minimal to moderate to elevated, plus the domain-specific areas worth reviewing. Its logic is deliberately conservative: it flags patterns that merit further review, but it does not declare a design lawful or unlawful.

"Unsure" is treated as a weak signal, so uncertainty becomes visible instead of disappearing. Co-occurring indicators raise the concern level, because risk mechanisms in monetisation tend to compound rather than act in isolation.

<img class="case-shot" src="/assets/showcases/case1/result.webp" alt="Tool result: an overall concern level with per-domain concern levels." width="1400" height="924" loading="lazy" />

I built the prototype by hand in HTML, CSS and vanilla JavaScript. The question set is kept separate from the interface logic, so the criteria can be updated as regulation, guidance and case law evolve.

## 05 · Impact

The project shows how a complex ethical and regulatory issue can become a repeatable, pre-launch screening step. Instead of relying on intuition or late legal review, teams get a shared structure to discuss monetisation risk, document design reasoning, and see where deeper review is needed.

The contribution is not a legal compliance tool, but a practical bridge between UX design, product decisions and consumer-protection expectations.

<blockquote class="case-quote">Built so a designer, a product owner and a legal reviewer can discuss the same flow with the same structure.</blockquote>

## 06 · Reflection

I prioritised research substance and assessment logic over interface polish. The tool works, but its visual hierarchy and result communication are the first things I would redesign in a next iteration.

The 12 questions are also a first version. As EU regulation and enforcement practice develop, the indicators and thresholds should be revisited. The honest limit matters: the tool can surface fairness risks and support better decisions, but it cannot replace legal judgement or resolve every ethical trade-off.
